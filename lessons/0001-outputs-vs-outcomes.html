Outputs vs Outcomes

The one distinction that stops a team shipping the wrong thing well.

Knowledge: 3 minQuiz: 4 minRewrite: 3 min

Why this, first

Your mission is to shape what your team builds so it stops shipping features that miss the real problem. The Institute of Digital Government's course puts it bluntly: projects "can struggle even when execution is strong, especially when they are solving the wrong problem" (Module 1). The fastest habit for catching that is to notice when a plan describes only what will be made and never what will change.

Knowledge

Output

A thing the team makes or delivers. A feature, a form, a report, a migration, a training session, a launch. Countable, and fully under the team's control.

Outcome

A change in what a named group of people do, or no longer have to do, once the output exists. Observable in the world, not in the backlog.

The IDG course frames success as "the change achieved, not the features delivered" (overview). Josh Seiden, whose short book Outcomes Over Output popularised the distinction, defines an outcome as "a change in human behavior that drives business results." Both definitions turn on the same thing: an outcome lives in what people do.

The test. Could we deliver this perfectly and still have nothing change for the people it is for?

If yes, it is an output. If the statement already says what changes for whom, it is an outcome.

Two quick supporting checks. Who is in the sentence? Outputs rarely name a user. Whose verb is it? "Ship", "build", "publish", "train" belong to the team. "Complete", "stop phoning", "reuse", "unblock themselves" belong to users.

Watch for outcome-sounding outputs. "100% of staff trained" is an output: training was delivered. "Staff make bookings in the new system instead of emailing admin" is the outcome it was meant to cause.

Skill: classify from memory

For each statement, decide whether it describes an output or an outcome. Answer before you scroll. You get feedback immediately.

Skill: rewrite an output as an outcome

Use the shape [Who] [does / no longer has to do what], [observable how]. Write your version first, then reveal a model answer and score yourself against the checklist.

Model: Parents get an accurate answer to routine questions within an hour, at any time of day, without a staff member typing it.

Note the outcome does not mention WhatsApp or a chatbot. Several different outputs could produce it, which is exactly why it is worth writing down first.

Model: Students studying in the evening keep working in the app instead of stopping or switching to another device.

This one is hard, and that is the point. If you struggled to find a credible outcome, the request may be a solution in search of a problem. That is the signal to ask what prompted it.

Model: Centre managers contact at-risk students within a week of a second missed session.

The report is an output. The contact is the outcome. A report nobody acts on hits the first and misses the second.

Checklist. Score each of your rewrites out of four.

  1. Names who changes.
  2. Says what they do differently, or no longer have to do.
  3. Could be observed without asking the team what it shipped.
  4. Could fail even if the output ships perfectly.

Carry it out of the room

Next time a request lands in your team's backlog, run the test on it silently. If it is an output, try the rewrite in your head before anyone discusses how to build it. Bring one real example to the next session and we will use it instead of a case study.

Read one thing

Primary source for this lesson: Outcomes Over Output by Josh Seiden. It is under a hundred pages and the first chapter alone covers today's distinction in more depth. If you only have five minutes, read IDG Module 1: Understanding the Problem.

Anything unclear? Ask your teacher in the terminal. Paste a statement from your own work and ask whether it is an output or an outcome, or ask for more practice items.
